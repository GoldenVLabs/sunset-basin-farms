The Problem

Most irrigation decisions are made from the surface

Farmers have always managed water with hard-won experience: how the soil feels in hand, how the crop looks in the morning, what the schedule and the water allotment allow. That knowledge works — it has kept farms running in the Klamath Basin through drought years and short water seasons.

But surface conditions only tell part of the story. Moisture at the root zone, soil temperature swings and salinity trends change below ground, days before they show up in the crop. Field sensors add that missing layer of information — so an irrigation set is confirmed by data, not just habit.

Technology supports the farmer's judgment—it does not replace it.
What We Monitor

Six things worth watching

Soil Moisture

Volumetric moisture at the root zone — see fields drying out before the crop shows stress, and confirm irrigation actually soaked in.

Soil Temperature

Ground temperature trends that drive germination timing, root activity and frost risk.

Electrical Conductivity

Tracks changes in dissolved salts and soil conditions that may help identify irrigation effects, salinity trends and shifts in the root zone.

Weather Conditions

Local weather conditions add important context to soil readings and irrigation decisions.

Battery & Signal Health

Every sensor reports its own battery voltage and radio signal, so a dying node gets replaced before data gaps appear.

Data-Delivery Status

Automated checks confirm readings are actually arriving — silence gets flagged, not ignored.

How It Works

From the field to your screen

  1. Field Sensors

    Rugged probes in the ground read moisture, temperature and conductivity around the clock.

  2. LoRaWAN Gateway

    Long-range, low-power radio carries readings across the farm — no field wiring, no Wi-Fi needed.

  3. Cellular or Internet Backhaul

    The gateway forwards data over a cellular or internet connection, built for remote fields with weak coverage.

  4. Live Dashboard & Alerts

    Current conditions and season-long trends on any phone or computer, with alerts when something needs attention.

Technical details for the curious

Sensors transmit on LoRaWAN (US915), chosen for multi-year battery life and mile-plus range in open terrain. The gateway backhauls over LTE where wired internet isn't available. Readings land in a time-series database and are visualized in dashboards with configurable alert thresholds for moisture, temperature, conductivity, battery voltage and missed check-ins. Everything is field-serviceable — the same hardware we run on our own farm.

Case Study

Built and proven at Sunset Basin Farms

Basin Roots wasn't designed in an office. Every part of the system was installed, broken, fixed and improved on our own 35+ acre family farm in Klamath Falls — through a drought year, a short water season and a Klamath Basin winter.

The Field Challenge

Flood-irrigated ground in a drought-prone basin, with limited water allotments and no way to see how deep each irrigation set actually soaked. We needed root-zone data to time sets with confidence.

Sensor Deployment

Multiple soil probes placed across the property — garden ground and open field — each reporting moisture, temperature and conductivity on a fixed interval.

Gateway Installation & Cellular Connectivity

The LoRaWAN gateway and antenna mounted on the barn roof, backhauling over cellular — the farm has no wired internet at the field, which is exactly the constraint most rural operations face.

Dashboard Output & Reliability Monitoring

A month of side-by-side sensor history: moisture response to irrigation, daily temperature swings, conductivity trends — and battery voltage tracked on every node so hardware problems surface before data gaps do.

Lessons Learned

A year of running the system on our own ground taught us what spec sheets don't:

— Sensor placement matters more than sensor count; one well-placed probe beats three careless ones.

— Battery and delivery monitoring aren't optional — a silent sensor is worse than no sensor.

— Farmers check dashboards from a tractor cab on a phone. If it isn't readable there, it isn't useful.
